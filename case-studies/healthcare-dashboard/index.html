Illustrative example

One dashboard for a healthcare services company

Not a client result · Healthcare services

Typical outcome Four spreadsheets become one morning screen, and the business is readable without a reconciliation meeting.

The challenge

Picture a multi-site healthcare services company running its weekly executive review on four spreadsheets — patient volume, staffing utilization, payer-mix, and cash collections — each maintained by a different person. By Monday morning the numbers are already two days old, the definitions do not always match, and a third of the meeting goes to reconciling the spreadsheets instead of making decisions.

The asks were simple but had been deferred for two years: one view of the business, refreshed automatically, with the same definitions used everywhere.

Our approach

The work starts by interviewing the four spreadsheet owners separately and asking the same question: "What does this number mean, and where does it come from?" The answers diverge in small but consequential ways — different inclusion rules for no-shows, different cutoffs for the reporting week, different handling of inter-site transfers. Before any code, the definitions go on one page and the person who owns the meeting signs off.

From there, the source systems — EHR exports, the staffing tool, and the practice management system — feed one place. The agreed definitions sit in a transformation layer. A scheduled monitor reads them on a clock and reports on its own, on a screen a person can actually read at a glance.

What we built

  • An automated nightly pipeline that pulls from the EHR, staffing system, and practice management software — replacing four manual spreadsheet refreshes.
  • A canonical metrics layer with documented definitions, so every number on the dashboard ties back to a written rule.
  • A single executive dashboard with [PLACEHOLDER: ~12] tiles covering volume, utilization, payer mix, and collections, plus a leaderboard view by site.
  • The same monitor flags any metric outside its expected range before the Monday meeting, so the room already knows what to ask. It reports. It does not send anything onward without a person.
  • A printable Friday PDF that goes to the board.

Typical outcome

  • Typical outcome: four weekly reports become one screen.
  • Typical outcome: the hours spent refreshing spreadsheets come back to the operations team.
  • Typical outcome: the Monday review is spent on decisions, not reconciliation.
  • Typical outcome: the numbers are ready overnight, not two days later.

Timeline

  • Week 1: Interviews with each spreadsheet owner, source system audit, and signed-off definitions document.
  • Weeks 2–3: Pipeline build from EHR, staffing, and PM systems into the warehouse; metrics layer in place.
  • Week 4: Dashboard build, side-by-side comparison against the legacy spreadsheets, cutover, and handoff. The spreadsheets are retired the same week.
